Swing path

Where the sensor actually went. Position is not double-integrated from acceleration — that drifts without bound and would be fiction within a second. The sensor is treated as a point on a rigid arm turning about a pivot, so position follows from orientation, integrated from the gyro, times a lever arm that is measured rather than assumed.

The tip path is a projection, not a measurement of the tip. Give the page a rod — its length, and the calibration holds — and it draws where the tip would be if the blank were rigid. The tip is the same rigid body as the sensor, further along the blank, so its position is orientation times a constant: nothing new is integrated and nothing new is fitted. What it is not is where the tip really went. A loaded fly rod bends by tens of centimetres through the stroke and springs back at the stop, and none of that is modelled here. Read it as the rod's aim drawn at a scale you can see — an 8° wrist break is 3 cm at the sensor and about 34 cm at the tip — rather than as the path the tip took.

Two consequences worth knowing. The tip sits about eleven times further from the pivot than the sensor, so it magnifies every orientation error by the same factor: one degree is 4 mm at the sensor and over 4 cm out there. That is why a burst with no still window before it draws its arm but refuses a tip — the sensor path survives an unanchored gravity reference, and the tip path would turn those few degrees into invented arc height. And the rod axis comes from two calibration holds whose disagreement is its error bar, shown in the rod row in centimetres at the tip; two holds that lean the same way agree perfectly and are both wrong, so a clean error bar is not proof of a clean axis.

Both views share one scale, so the overhead view reading as a near-straight line is the finding rather than a rendering choice — the swing barely leaves its plane. Grid squares are 10 cm. The two views point the same way: seen from above the casting axis runs up the page, and in the side elevation forward is to the left, so a forward cast sweeps up the page from above and leftward from the side.

Which way is forward is assumed, not measured. Gravity fixes which way is up but leaves the heading free, so nothing in a handle-mounted IMU says which end of the swing is in front of you. Both views assume the first travelling stroke of a burst is a pickup and therefore goes back. When that is wrong — a burst that opens on a forward cast, say — Flip forward corrects it, and it turns both views together rather than leaving them disagreeing. No measurement anywhere in the report depends on the choice.

Colour is which way each stroke turned, measured about the burst's own rotation axis. In casting the colours should alternate, because a back cast and the forward cast after it are one movement run in opposite directions — so two of the same colour in a row means either a missed stroke or a movement that wasn't a cast, and is worth looking at rather than explaining away.

Residual is the share of dynamic acceleration the rigid-arm model fails to explain — lower is a better fit. A burst that never turned fast enough to identify the lever arm is listed but not drawn.

Consistency

The headline is 100% minus the stroke-to-stroke spread across arc, duration, peak rate and stop deceleration, so higher means your strokes repeat each other more closely. Planarity and peak acceleration are excluded: they track the mount and geometry more than technique. This is repeatability, not accuracy — the sensor has no target to measure you against, so a session can be 95% consistent and consistently wrong.
